How the gravity filter works

The estimator that turns the IMU's gyro and accelerometer readings into tilt for the balance controller. It runs in the control task once per IMU sample, at 960 Hz, and is implemented in firmware/src/est/GravityFilter.cpp.

IMU and filtering terms are defined in the glossary at the end.

Summary

  • State: the gravity direction in body axes, a unit vector u, plus the gyro bias b.
  • Propagate u with the bias-corrected gyro rate.
  • Correct it towards the gravity direction measured by the accelerometer, through a PI law. The P term (k_p = 1 rad/s) removes tilt error; the I term (k_i = 0.1 rad/s²) estimates the gyro bias.
  • Before it's used, the accelerometer reading has the robot's own motion subtracted (rigid-body lever-arm terms and pivot acceleration). It is also weighted by how close its magnitude is to 1 g.
  • In one axis this is a complementary filter with a crossover near 0.16 Hz: gyro above it, accelerometer below.

The problem

The IMU (an LSM6DSV16X on the controller board) contains two sensors, sampled together at 960 Hz. Neither can give tilt on its own.

Gyro: angular rate ω

Measures rotation rate about the three body axes. Integrating it gives the change in orientation, with very little noise and no lag.

Every gyro has a slowly varying offset, the bias. Integration turns that into a growing angle error (drift): 0.02 rad/s of bias is 1.15° per second. The gyro is good at high frequencies and useless at DC.

Accelerometer: specific force f

An accelerometer doesn't measure acceleration. It measures specific force, f = a − g: acceleration minus the gravity vector. At rest it reads 1 g pointing up; in free fall it reads zero. So when the sensor isn't accelerating, f gives the up direction directly, with no drift.

Any real acceleration of the sensor point adds to that reading and tilts the apparent "up". The accelerometer is good at DC and corrupted at higher frequencies.

Their error spectra are complementary, so the standard approach is to blend them: trust the gyro for fast changes and the accelerometer for the slow average. This filter does that. It also reduces the accelerometer's corruption by predicting and subtracting the acceleration it can work out from the gyro and the geometry.

State and outputs

Body axes: x forward, y along the axle to the left, z up (right-handed). The filter's main state is u, world "up" expressed in body axes, kept at unit length. Tilt θ is rotation about +y, positive when the top leans forward:

Why a vector rather than angles or a quaternion. Euler angles (roll/pitch/yaw) have singularities, poses where the representation breaks down; this robot passes through all of them because it goes upside down. A quaternion avoids that, but it also carries heading. Heading can't be corrected without a magnetometer, so the filter would be carrying a state it can't observe. The up-vector holds exactly the two degrees of freedom gravity can observe (tilt and side lean), and is valid at every pose. Angles are computed from it only at the output.

Propagation: what the gyro does

World up is fixed in the world. Seen from a body rotating at ω, a world-fixed vector rotates at −ω:

du/dt = −ω × u

The filter integrates this with one Euler step per sample, then renormalizes u to unit length so rounding can't change its length:

u ← normalize(u − dt · (ω × u))

dt is taken from the IMU's own sample timestamps (48 ticks of its internal clock per sample, scaled by the chip's measured clock error). A late or skipped sample is therefore integrated over the right interval. Run on its own, this step tracks motion perfectly apart from the integrated bias, which is what the correction removes.

One update

  1. Bias-correct the gyro. ω = ω_m − b
  2. Predict the non-gravity specific force at the IMU from the body's motion. This is the part of the accelerometer reading that isn't gravity. See lever-arm compensation. f_pred = a_ref + α × r + ω × (ω × r)
  3. Measured up direction: remove the prediction; what remains should be gravity's specific force, 1 g up. d = f_m − f_pred, û = d / |d|
  4. Accelerometer trust: a magnitude check on d (see below). w = clamp(1 − | |d| − g | / 0.2g, 0, 1)
  5. Error: the cross product of measured and estimated up. For small errors it's the rotation vector that takes u onto û: its direction is the rotation axis and its magnitude is sin of the angle between them. e = û × u
  6. PI correction: the proportional term goes into the rate used for propagation, and the integral term updates the bias. ω_c = ω + k_p·w·e, b ← clamp(b − k_i·w·e·dt, ±0.1 rad/s)
  7. Propagate with the corrected rate. u ← normalize(u − dt·(ω_c × u))

The correction law and its tuning

Error dynamics. Linearise about a pose, in the tilt axis only. Let x be the tilt error and β the true gyro bias, and assume full trust. Then:

ẋ = (β − b) − k_p·x ḃ = k_i·x ⇒ ẍ + k_p·ẋ + k_i·x = 0

With k_p = 1 rad/s and k_i = 0.1 rad/s² the poles are at −0.11 and −0.89 rad/s:

  • the fast pole (τ ≈ 1.1 s) pulls a tilt error out;
  • the slow pole (τ ≈ 9 s) is the bias being learned.

A constant gyro bias produces a transient tilt error that peaks at roughly β/k_p, then decays to zero as b converges to β. The spec's starting value, k_i = 0.01, puts the slow pole at −0.01 rad/s (τ ≈ 100 s). That leaves 0.86° of error 30 s after a 0.02 rad/s bias appears, against a requirement of 0.2°, so k_i was raised to 0.1.

Complementary-filter view. Treat the accelerometer-derived tilt θ_a as an input alongside the gyro rate. The same loop gives:

θ̂(s) = s² / (s² + k_p·s + k_i) · ω(s)/s gyro, integrated + (k_p·s + k_i) / (s² + k_p·s + k_i) · θ_a(s) accelerometer

The two transfer functions sum to 1, which is what makes it a complementary filter. The gyro is high-passed and the accelerometer low-passed, crossing over near k_p = 1 rad/s (0.16 Hz). Accelerometer disturbances faster than about a second are heavily attenuated. Gyro errors slower than that, including bias (handled by the integrator), are removed. The control loop's tilt rate θ̇ is the raw bias-corrected gyro, so it has no filter lag at all.

Yaw bias is unobservable. Rotation about the gravity vector doesn't change u, so e never has a component along u and the bias about the vertical is never corrected. Heading ψ is therefore an open-loop integral and drifts.

Accelerometer trust weighting

This is a heuristic. An acceleration perpendicular to gravity changes the magnitude only to second order, so a sustained sideways acceleration can still get through partly. That case is what the motion prediction below is for.

Lever-arm compensation

The IMU is 56.5 mm above the axle, so when the body pitches, the sensor moves on an arc. For a rigid body, the acceleration of a point P at offset r from a reference point O is:

a_P = a_O + α × r + ω × (ω × r) pivot tangential centripetal

The filter evaluates this with ω and α from the gyro and adds the pivot's own acceleration a_ref. The result, f_pred, is subtracted from the accelerometer reading before the reading is used as a gravity measurement. Without this step, swinging the body would show up as a tilt error.

Angular acceleration. The gyro gives ω, not α. α is the sample-to-sample difference of ω divided by dt, passed through a first-order low-pass at 40 Hz. Raw differencing would amplify gyro noise by roughly 1/dt. The low-pass costs about 4 ms of lag, which is small against the body's motion (a few Hz).

Effect. In simulation with the real geometry, swinging ±10° at 1.5 Hz about the axle gives a peak tilt error of 0.79° without compensation and 0.06° with it.

Pivot and contact mode: wheels or rings

The pivot O, and its acceleration a_ref, depend on what's touching the floor. The filter tracks this as the contact mode, decided from θ.

The wheels touch the floor while d_c·cos θ + r_w ≥ h(θ), where h(θ) is the ring centre's height above the floor (defined below). For this geometry that holds for |θ| < θ_c ≈ 20.3°. The mode switches at θ_c with ±2° hysteresis.

Wheel mode, |θ| < θ_c

Pivot: the axle. r = r_imu = (0, 0, 56.5 mm).

a_ref is the axle's acceleration: v̇·h + v·ψ̇·(u × h). Here v is the ground speed from the wheel encoders and h is the horizontal forward direction in body axes. The second term is the centripetal acceleration of a turn.

Until the drivers report wheel speed (stage 4), v = 0. That's exact for the stage 3 swing test, where the wheels are held.

Ring mode, |θ| > θ_c

Pivot: the ring centre. r = r_imu − (0, 0, d_c) = (0, 0, 8.5 mm).

a_ref comes from rolling without slip on a non-circular ring (below).

Rolling kinematics. For a convex shape rolling on a flat floor, define h(θ) as the height of the reference point (here the ring centre) above the floor at tilt θ. In geometry this is the shape's support function. With no slip, the contact point is the instantaneous centre of rotation. The centre therefore moves forward at h·θ̇ and vertically at h′·θ̇, which gives:

a_ref = (h·θ̈ + h′·θ̇²) · forward + (h′·θ̈ + h″·θ̇²) · up

with θ̇ = ω_y and θ̈ = α_y. For a circle of radius R this reduces to R·θ̈ forward. For the ellipse, with half-width a = 67.5 mm and half-height b = 75 mm:

h(θ) = √(a²·sin²θ + b²·cos²θ) 75 mm on the ends, 67.5 mm on the sides

Near upright, the ellipse's lowest point falls inside the 66 mm cut-out. The ring then rests on one of the two corners the cut leaves (25 mm ahead of or behind the axle, 22 mm below it), and h becomes that corner's height. That's a fixed point pivoting, so h″ = −h. The firmware picks whichever case applies at each θ.

Effect. Rolling ±40° at 0.8 Hz on the rings, in simulation, gives a peak error of 1.87° without compensation and 0.12° with it.

Mounting alignment: zero

The IMU board may be fitted a little rotated in pitch relative to the body frame defined by the CAD. The console command zero measures and stores that offset:

  1. Hold the robot in the reference pose (ring centre directly above the axle) and run zero.
  2. The current θ is added to a stored mounting angle, which is saved in flash.
  3. From then on, every gyro, accelerometer and SFLP vector is rotated about y by that angle before it enters the filter. The filter state is rotated by the same increment at that moment, so the output doesn't jump.

Pitch only. It corrects sensor alignment, not the balance point: if the centre of mass isn't exactly on the axle-to-ring-centre line, that offset is the controller's to find.

Relation to the Mahony filter

This is a variant of the Mahony filter (Mahony, Hamel and Pflimlin, 2008). It's the standard explicit complementary filter for attitude: a cross-product error between measured and estimated reference directions, fed back through a PI law into the rate that drives the attitude integration. The correction step above is exactly Mahony's. The differences:

Textbook MahonyHereReason
Integrates a full attitude quaternionIntegrates only the gravity vector u; heading integrated separatelyNo magnetometer, so heading is unobservable. The vector form has no singularity, and the robot runs inverted.
Treats the accelerometer as a pure gravity sensorSubtracts predicted rigid-body and pivot acceleration firstThe IMU is off the pivot: 0.79° → 0.06° (swing), 1.87° → 0.12° (rolling)
Constant correction gainGain scaled by trust w from |d|Suppresses correction during unmodelled acceleration; a common extension
Unbounded integratorBias clamped to ±0.1 rad/s per axisAnti-windup during long disturbances

Why not something else? The common alternatives are the Madgwick filter (gradient-descent correction, otherwise similar) and an extended Kalman filter. The EKF would need process and measurement noise covariances to be modelled and tuned. Its gains would vary with that model, and it costs considerably more per sample. For a two-degree-of-freedom gravity problem at 960 Hz, Mahony gives the same structure (fast gyro path, slow accelerometer correction, bias estimation) with two gains whose effect is easy to understand.

Limitations

  • Heading drifts. No absolute yaw reference.
  • Yaw-axis bias isn't estimated (unobservable from gravity).
  • Unmodelled acceleration (impacts, and driving until wheel odometry arrives in stage 4) is handled only by the trust weighting.
  • Tilt accuracy depends on geometry accuracy. r_imu, d_c and the ring shape come from CAD; wrong values make the compensation subtract the wrong force.

The IMU's on-chip fusion (SFLP) runs alongside. Its gravity vector gives an independent tilt that is logged as a cross-check and never used for control.

Glossary

IMU
Inertial measurement unit: a gyro and accelerometer in one package. Here an ST LSM6DSV16X, sampled at 960 Hz (its output data rate, ODR).
Specific force
What an accelerometer actually measures: acceleration minus gravity, f = a − g. It reads +1 g up at rest and zero in free fall.
Bias
A slowly varying offset in a sensor's output. For a gyro, a non-zero reading when stationary.
Drift
The growing orientation error from integrating a biased rate.
Attitude
Orientation relative to the world, especially relative to gravity (tilt and side lean). Heading is the remaining degree of freedom.
Body / world frame
Axes fixed to the robot versus axes fixed to the ground. The filter works in body axes.
Euler angles
Orientation as three successive rotations (roll, pitch, yaw). They have singularities ("gimbal lock"), where two rotations line up and the angles become undefined or jump.
Quaternion
A four-number representation of full 3D orientation with no singularities. Standard for attitude filters that also track heading.
Observability
Whether a state can be inferred from the measurements. Rotation about gravity isn't observable from an accelerometer, so heading and yaw-axis bias aren't.
Complementary filter
Fuses two sensors with complementary error spectra: a high-pass on one plus a low-pass on the other, summing to 1.
Mahony filter
A nonlinear complementary filter for attitude using a cross-product error and PI feedback into the gyro rate (Mahony, Hamel, Pflimlin, 2008).
Madgwick filter
A similar attitude filter that corrects by gradient descent instead of a cross-product error.
EKF
Extended Kalman filter: a model-based estimator that weights sensors by modelled noise covariances. More general, more to tune.
Lever-arm compensation
Removing the accelerometer reading caused by the sensor being offset from the point the body rotates about.
Accelerometer gating / trust
Reducing the accelerometer's weight when its magnitude shows it isn't measuring gravity alone.
Support function
For a convex shape, the distance from a reference point to a supporting line with a given normal. Here: the ring centre's height above the floor as a function of tilt.
Contact mode
Whether the robot stands on its wheels (pivot at the axle) or rolls on its rings (pivot at the ring centre).
SFLP
"Sensor fusion low power": the IMU's built-in orientation filter. Used here only as a cross-check.
Mounting alignment
The angular offset between the IMU's axes and the body frame. Corrected in pitch by zero.
